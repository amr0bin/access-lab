# Living as the Advisor
## A "future self" practice for the Accessibility Advisor transition

*Written 5 October 2026. A companion to `competitiveness-plan-accessibility-advising.md` and `two-year-front-facing-experience-plan.md`. Those plans say what to get onto the record. This one says how to live, in the meantime, as the person who already holds the role. It adds rooms, reading and rhythms to the existing moves; it does not add a sixth commitment.*

---

## 1. Who I am becoming — the ceiling

**October 2028.** I am an Accessibility Advisor in a BC post-secondary accessibility office — the UBC Centre for Accessibility role, or the SFU Disability Access Advisor role in the School of Medicine, where the essential-requirements work is the job itself. I make accommodation decisions with confidence because I have been reading documentation, case law and policy like an advisor for two years before the title arrived.

I am also known beyond my own office. I have presented at AHEAD or CACUSS on what BC universities ask of the people who decide accommodation. My corpus study on essential requirements is out as a preprint and cited in conversations about clinical and placement education. I sit on a statutory accessibility committee. I am in year two of the Leeds MSc, writing from practice rather than towards it. When a unit in this province has a hard question about essential requirements in clinical programmes, my name comes up.

**The ceiling past that:** a policy-and-change role leading institutional accessibility planning, or a national voice on essential requirements and access in clinical education — the place where fifteen years of learning design, disability studies, and accommodation practice meet and almost nobody else stands.

Everything below is built to make the 2028 person real early.

---

## 2. The principle: act at the level of the role, in its rooms, with its texts

Living as if the change has already happened means three things, in practice:

1. **Be in the rooms advisors are in** — their conferences, webinars, listservs and local events — so the field's vocabulary, current debates and people become familiar before the interview.
2. **Do the advisor's thinking weekly** — read the cases, policies and documentation guidance an advisor reads, and reason through determinations on paper.
3. **Carry the advisor's markers** — memberships, a bookshelf, a practice log, a way of introducing yourself — so others start to place you in the role too.

| Prevention question | Promotion question |
|---|---|
| Is attending conferences before I hold the role worth the money and time? | What would it make possible to already know the people who will sit on the panel, and to have presented to them? |
| Could reading advisor-level material before I do the job be premature? | What would it take to walk into the first week of an advisor role already fluent in LR7, GP 26, *Meiorin* and the functional-limitations model? |
| Will this stretch the two-commitments rule? | Which of these rooms and rhythms turn the existing commitments (CPACC, crisis line, DABC, committee) into visible, connected expertise rather than separate lines on a CV? |

The answer to the last pair is the design rule for this document: everything here either **feeds an existing move** or **makes it visible**.

## 5. What future-me reads — the advisor's bookshelf

Sequenced by quarter so the reading runs alongside the moves. One book or instrument cluster at a time.

**Q4 2026 — the instruments an advisor decides with**
- UBC Policy LR7; SFU GP 26 and GP 40; the BC Human Rights Code; the *Accessible British Columbia Act*.
- *British Columbia (Public Service Employee Relations Commission) v. BCGSEU* (*Meiorin*, 1999) and *Moore v. British Columbia (Education)* (2012). Read the decisions, not summaries.
- AHEAD's documentation guidance and the CAS Standards for disability resources.

**Q1 2027 — the practice**
- Sheryl Burgstahler, ed., *Universal Design in Higher Education: From Principles to Practice* (2nd ed.).
- Anne Meyer, David Rose and David Gordon, *Universal Design for Learning: Theory and Practice*.
- One BC Human Rights Tribunal accommodation-in-education decision a week (CanLII), read as an advisor: what did the institution do, where did the process hold or fail?

**Q2 2027 — the critical frame (doubles as Leeds pre-reading)**
- Jay Timothy Dolmage, *Academic Ableism: Disability and Higher Education*.
- Margaret Price, *Mad at School* and *Crip Spacetime*. *Mad at School* speaks directly to the mental-health accommodations that dominate every caseload.
- Lennard Davis, ed., *The Disability Studies Reader* (already on the Leeds list).

**Q3 2027 — the community voice**
- Alice Wong, ed., *Disability Visibility*.
- Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha, *Care Work*.
- Alison Kafer, *Feminist, Queer, Crip*.

Each book gets one short practice-voice note on the site: *what this changes about how I would decide an accommodation.* That is how reading becomes visible, current expertise.

## 6. How future-me works — weekly rhythms

These are small, and they are what make the identity real between events.

- **The advisor's hour (weekly).** One case, one policy clause, one hypothetical. Write a half-page determination: request, documentation, functional limitation, essential requirement at stake, accommodation, reasoning. Keep these in a casebook. By month twelve there are forty, and the interview scenario question has already been answered forty times.
- **The practice log (from the two-year plan).** Every DABC session, crisis-line shift, committee meeting and Make Space cohort, logged in the postings' vocabulary.
- **Listening to the field.** Subscribe to AHEAD's and NEADS's newsletters, follow BC accessibility-office news, and read the posting language each time a new role appears. Fluency in the field's current conversation is itself a marker.
- **Introduce yourself as the person you are becoming.** "I work in accessibility and inclusive learning design, focused on accommodation and essential requirements in post-secondary." True now; it will be the job title soon.

## 8. What multiplies this — people, money, structures

- **One mentor inside a BC unit.** After the three informational conversations, ask the most generous person whether you can check in once a quarter. Mentors in disability services often become the panel member who says "I know her work."
- **A co-presenter.** Proposing with someone inside an office (AHEAD, CACUSS) is faster entry than proposing alone, and the presentation itself becomes a working relationship.
- **Funding for the rooms.** Presenter rates usually cut registration costs; ask every conference about early-career, presenter or volunteer rates; apply for the CDSA bursary; and if the next role (Move 1 in the competitiveness plan) is at a university or college, its professional-development fund is the obvious source for AHEAD 2027 travel. Raise it at the offer stage.
- **The committee as a classroom.** An Accessible BC Act committee seat is a monthly room full of the people who shape institutional accessibility plans. Treat it as the most local conference.
- **A peer cohort.** Find one or two others making the same move (NEADS, CDSA, or the CPACC study community) and meet monthly to trade cases and postings.

## 10. How to know it's working

By July 2027: people in BC accessibility offices recognise your name before they see your CV; you have a casebook of forty reasoned determinations; you have been in the room — or on the programme — at the field's flagship conference; and when the next advisor posting appears, the application reads as a colleague's, not an outsider's.
