Twelve Tuesdays, one exam, then stop

The Certified Professional in Accessibility Core Competencies, from the International Association of Accessibility Professionals. Neither advisor posting names it; its value is a dated, external marker, bought cheaply. There is no prize for a high score. Each week below is one Tuesday, weighted to the exam's own split: 40% disabilities and assistive technology, 40% accessibility and universal design, 20% standards, laws and management.

The source, and how to use it

The plan follows the CPACC Body of Knowledge, October 2023, version 4.0, which is in the Accessible Learning Labs project as CPACCBoK.pdf. Page numbers below are that document's own. Two things follow from its date. It teaches web accessibility from WCAG 2.1, so study 2.1 for the exam even though 2.2 is the current W3C Recommendation. And its national instruments are the Equality Act 2010, the ADA and the AODA: the Accessible Canada Act and the Accessible BC Act are not in it, and are studied for the job, not for the exam.

Three tools, kept for the whole twelve weeks:

  • The error log. One line per wrong practice answer: the section, what I chose, what was right, and why I chose wrong. The why is the useful column. Review it every Tuesday before new material.
  • Practice questions. IAAP's own materials first. Verify what the new IAAP Education Portal, announced for August 2026, includes, and whether it is free to members. Third-party banks are useful for volume and unreliable for accuracy; check any answer that contradicts the Body of Knowledge against the Body of Knowledge.
  • The fifteen minutes. Ten questions or the flashcards on each of the other six days. This is what turns one long Tuesday into spaced practice.

Ticks on this page are kept in this browser only. The record of what was done lives in access-lab-record.md.

Orientation, and the models of disability

Read

Sections 1 to 5 (pp. 1–3): what the exam measures and how the outline is weighted. Domain One A, the theoretical models: medical, social, biopsychosocial, economic, functional solutions, social identity (pp. 4–8).

Practise

A short diagnostic set across all three domains, untimed, before studying further. It shows where the weight actually needs to go.

Done means

The error log exists. Each model can be told from the others in one sentence.

Categories of disability, and sensory disabilities

Read

Domain One B, categories, barriers and solutions (p. 9). Domain One C: visual disabilities, auditory disabilities, deaf-blindness (pp. 10–15).

Practise

Questions on One A to One C so far. Match each barrier to the assistive technology that addresses it.

Done means

Screen readers, magnification, braille displays, captioning, sign language interpretation and CART placed against the right barriers.

Speech, language, mobility and body structure

Read

Domain One C: speech and language disabilities (pp. 15–18); mobility, flexibility and body structure (pp. 18–20).

Practise

This week's sections, then the error log from weeks 1 and 2.

Done means

AAC, switch access, alternative input and voice recognition distinguished and matched to barriers.

Cognitive disabilities

Read

Domain One C: cognitive disabilities (pp. 20–27). The longest single section in the document, so it has the whole week.

Practise

Questions on cognitive disabilities only. Watch for the Body of Knowledge's own terminology, which is not always the disability-studies register used elsewhere in this practice.

Done means

The subcategories held apart, each with its typical barriers and supports.

Seizure, psychological and complex disabilities; data; etiquette

Read

Domain One C: seizure, psychological, multiple and complex (pp. 27–33). One D, data trends and implications (pp. 33–35). One E, disability etiquette (p. 35).

Practise

This week's sections. Note the prevalence figures the Body of Knowledge cites; the exam draws on its numbers, not newer ones.

Done means

Domain One read once through.

Domain One review, and accommodation versus universal design

Read

Morning: the whole Domain One error log. Afternoon: Domain Two A, individualised accommodation versus inclusive design; Two B, the benefits of accessibility (pp. 36–38).

Practise

A timed block of forty Domain One questions, at the exam's pace of about 72 seconds a question.

Done means

Domain One scoring comfortably above the pass line on the timed block.

WCAG and the built environment

Read

Domain Two C, web accessibility principles from WCAG 2.1 (pp. 39–41). Two D, the built environment (pp. 41–42).

Practise

POUR, the conformance levels, and which success criteria belong to which principle.

Done means

Also today: the baseline ledger review. Twenty minutes at the end of the day.

Universal design and UDL · apply for the exam

Read

Domain Two E, the principles of universal design (pp. 42–44). Two F, universal design for learning, with usability and user experience (pp. 44–47). Home ground, which is exactly why it gets read carefully: the exam wants the Body of Knowledge's version, not mine.

Practise

Domain Two questions throughout.

Done means

The IAAP application submitted today, with any testing accommodations requested on it, so approval clears before a December sitting.

International and regional instruments

Read

Domain Two review in the morning. Domain Three A: the Universal Declaration, the CRPD, the Marrakesh Treaty (pp. 48–51). Three B: the EU Charter, the African Charter, the Inter-American Convention (pp. 52–55).

Practise

Instrument, year, and what each one obliges. The CRPD articles that matter for education and work (24 and 27) are also the Leeds human-rights module.

Done means

Domain Two timed block passed; Three A and B read.

National law, domain-specific law and procurement

Read

Domain Three C: the Equality Act 2010, the ADA, the AODA, EU member-state laws (pp. 55–58). Three D, domain-specific and procurement laws (pp. 59–60).

Practise

Which law, which jurisdiction, which obligation. The UK competence-standards carve-out in the Equality Act is also the reading the four-literature paper needs.

Done means

Three C and D read.

ICT standards and accessibility across an organisation

Read

Domain Three E, applying standards to ICT (pp. 61–63). Three F, integrating accessibility across the organisation: maturity models, champions, evaluation, hiring, communication, legal exposure, procurement (pp. 64–70).

Practise

Domain Three timed block of twenty.

Done means

All three domains read once through.

The mock, and the weakest domain

Read

Morning: one full mock, 100 questions in 120 minutes, in exam conditions. Afternoon: the error log, sorted by domain, and the weakest domain reread.

Practise

Nothing new. Skim the Additional Reading lists (pp. 71–72) only for names already met; do not start new reading this week.

Done means

The mock passed with room to spare. If not, sit in the first week of January instead and give the weakest domain two more Tuesdays.

Where it sits

The CPACC closes most of the postings' knowledge lines, and nothing else. It does not teach how an advisor reads documentation and decides; that is on The Role. After the exam, the Tuesday hours it held go to Leeds and the corpus, and this page becomes a record.
